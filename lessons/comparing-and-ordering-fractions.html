Comparing and Ordering Fractions

Driving Question: How can we tell which fraction is greater when they look so different?

Explore one way at a time: open a section below. Opening another Way of Knowing will close the one you were using.

Learning pathway: KS3 Foundations in Number — comparing and ordering fractions using visual models, equivalent fractions, common denominators and benchmark values.

Seeds of Change: compare and order fractions with the same or different denominators; use equivalent fractions and common denominators; compare fractions with benchmarks such as 0, 1/2 and 1; place fractions on a number line; and justify comparisons clearly.

🧠 The Knowing World

How can fractions be compared?

Fractions can be compared in several ways. A strong mathematician chooses the method that makes the comparison easiest to see.

  • Same denominator: compare the numerators.
  • Same numerator: the fraction with the smaller denominator is larger.
  • Benchmark values: compare each fraction with 0, 1/2 or 1.
  • Equivalent fractions: rewrite both fractions using a common denominator.
  • Number lines: the fraction further to the right is greater.

Example: 3/4 and 5/8 can be rewritten as 6/8 and 5/8, so 3/4 > 5/8.

Key vocabulary

🌍 The Human Story

Fair Shares at the Community Celebration

At a community celebration, groups may receive different fractions of identical cakes, trays or activity time. Deciding whether the shares are fair requires comparing the actual amounts, not only the number of pieces.

Group A receives 3/5 of a cake. Group B receives 5/8 of an identical cake. Which group receives more?

Fairness questions

  • Does a larger numerator always mean a larger fraction?
  • Could more pieces still represent a smaller amount?
  • What mathematical evidence would make the decision feel fair?

🌱 The Living Body

Pause before choosing a method

When fractions look different, it can feel as though you need a long calculation. First pause and look for an easier comparison.

  1. Are the denominators the same?
  2. Are the numerators the same?
  3. Can I compare each fraction with 1/2 or 1?
  4. Would a common denominator make the comparison clearer?

Confidence check

  • “I can compare fractions using a visual model.”
  • “I can choose between benchmarks and common denominators.”
  • “I can order several fractions and justify my decisions.”

Helpful self-talk

“I do not need to use the same method every time. I can choose the comparison that makes the fractions easiest to see.”

Reflection

🛠 Final Task

Assignment: Create a Fraction Comparison Guide

Design a poster, report, slide or digital guide that teaches another learner how to compare and order fractions.

Your final product must include:

  • one comparison using the same denominator
  • one comparison using the same numerator
  • one comparison using a benchmark such as 1/2 or 1
  • one comparison using a common denominator
  • at least four fractions ordered from least to greatest
  • a number-line or fraction-bar model
  • a written explanation of the hardest comparison
  • a short reflection on which strategy was most efficient

KS3 readiness

This task prepares learners to compare rational numbers, choose efficient representations, use equivalence flexibly and justify numerical order.

Final Task Rubric

Criteria Emerging Developing Secure Extending
Conceptual Understanding I can compare some fractions using a visual model and support. I can use familiar benchmarks or equivalent fractions with prompts. I can explain why one fraction is greater and choose an appropriate comparison method. I can compare methods and justify which is most efficient for different fraction pairs.
Fluency & Accuracy I can order familiar fractions with support. I can compare and order fractions, though I may make occasional equivalence errors. I can accurately compare and order fractions using benchmarks and common denominators. I can work efficiently with close or less familiar fractions and check using another method.
Application to Problems I can attempt a familiar sharing problem when a model is provided. I can use comparison in familiar fairness or measure contexts with some support. I can apply fraction comparison to practical problems and explain whether a decision is fair. I can design, test and justify my own fraction-ranking situation.
Independence & Reflection I can begin with support and identify when I need help. I can use a suggested model or method and respond to feedback. I can choose a method, check my order and explain what helped me learn. I can work independently, refine my reasoning and compare several valid strategies.
