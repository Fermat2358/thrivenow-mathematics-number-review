KS3 Foundations in Number

Finding Percentages of Amounts

Driving Question: How can knowing a few simple percentages help us find almost any percentage of an amount?

Explore one way at a time: open a section below. Opening another Way of Knowing will close the one you were using.

Learning pathway: KS3 Foundations in Number — percentages within the connected study of fractions, decimals and percentages.

Seeds of Change: understand percentages as parts per hundred; recall common fraction, decimal and percentage equivalents; find 1%, 10%, 20%, 25%, 50% and 75% of quantities; combine known percentages to find less familiar percentages; and solve practical problems involving money, measures and quantities.

🧠 The Knowing World

What does percentage mean?

Percentage means out of 100. A percentage describes a proportion of a whole.

Percentage Fraction Decimal Useful method
50% 1/2 0.5 Divide by 2
25% 1/4 0.25 Divide by 4
10% 1/10 0.1 Divide by 10
1% 1/100 0.01 Divide by 100

Building less familiar percentages

Familiar percentages can be combined to create other percentages.

35% of 240
30% of 240 = 72
5% of 240 = 12
Therefore, 35% of 240 = 84.

Another method is to find 1% first. For example, 1% of 350 is 3.5, so 18% is 18 × 3.5.

Key vocabulary

percentage, proportion, whole, amount, quantity, discount, donation, equivalent, decompose, unitary method

🌍 The Human Story

Percentages in markets and communities

Percentages are used to describe discounts, donations, profits, stock levels and participation. Clear percentage information helps people make informed and fair decisions.

A market stall offers 25% off a £48 item. The discount is £12, but the new price is £36. These are related quantities, but they are not the same answer.

Fairness and communication

  • Could a discount sign be mathematically correct but still confusing?
  • Why should a charity explain how much of its income is donated?
  • How can percentage calculations help communities plan resources?

🌱 The Living Body

Pause before calculating

A percentage problem can look complicated before it is broken into familiar parts. Pause and choose a starting percentage you know.

  1. Identify the whole amount.
  2. Identify the required percentage.
  3. Choose a useful building block: 50%, 25%, 10%, 5% or 1%.
  4. Combine the parts carefully.
  5. Check whether the answer is a sensible proportion of the whole.

Confidence check

  • “I can find familiar percentages such as 10%, 25% and 50%.”
  • “I can combine known percentages to find a new percentage.”
  • “I can solve a percentage problem and explain why my answer is reasonable.”

Helpful self-talk

“I do not need one special rule for every percentage. I can build the answer from percentages I already know.”

KS3 readiness

This task prepares learners to use percentages as multiplicative operators, solve financial and proportional problems and justify efficient calculation strategies.

Final Task Rubric

Criteria Emerging Developing Secure Extending
Conceptual Understanding I can recognise some familiar percentages using a visual model. I can connect familiar percentages to fractions and decimals with some support. I can explain percentage as a proportion of a whole and show how less familiar percentages are built. I can compare methods and generalise efficient percentage strategies.
Fluency & Accuracy I can find some simple percentages with a model or prompt. I can calculate familiar percentages, though I may make errors when combining parts. I can accurately calculate familiar and less familiar percentages using efficient methods. I can work accurately with decimal answers and verify results using another method.
Application to Problems I can attempt a familiar percentage problem when the method is given. I can solve familiar discount or quantity problems with some support. I can select a suitable method, solve practical problems and distinguish the percentage amount from the final total. I can design, compare and evaluate multi-step percentage situations.
Independence & Reflection I can begin with support and identify when I need help. I can use a suggested model or strategy and respond to feedback. I can choose a strategy, check my answer and explain what helped me succeed. I can work independently, refine a solution and justify why one method is especially efficient.
