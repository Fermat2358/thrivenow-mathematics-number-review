🧠 The Knowing World

What do the four operations do?

Operation What it can represent Useful words
Addition + Combining or increasing total, altogether, sum, more
Subtraction − Taking away, comparing or finding a difference remaining, difference, fewer, change
Multiplication × Equal groups or repeated scaling groups of, each, product, times
Division ÷ Sharing equally or finding how many groups share, per, each, groups, quotient

Inverse relationships

Addition and subtraction are inverse operations. Multiplication and division are inverse operations. Inverses help us check calculations.

If 4,286 + 3,719 = 8,005, then 8,005 − 3,719 should equal 4,286.

Choosing an efficient method

  • Use a mental method when the numbers have a helpful structure.
  • Use a written method when accuracy across several place-value columns is needed.
  • Estimate before calculating so you know roughly what answer to expect.
  • Check using an inverse operation or a different method.

Quick diagnostic questions

  1. Which operation would find the difference between 8,500 and 6,275?
  2. Which operation would find the cost of 36 tickets at £14 each?
  3. Which operation would share 1,248 bottles equally between 12 stalls?
  4. Estimate 4,982 + 3,104 by rounding each number to the nearest thousand.
🌍 The Human Story

Mathematics behind a community event

Community events involve many connected decisions: buying food, hiring equipment, selling tickets, dividing supplies fairly and making sure spending does not exceed the available budget.

The operation chosen can affect whether plans are accurate and fair. For example, confusing the total number of drinks with the number required for each table could lead to shortages or wasted money.

Fairness inquiry

A community event has 720 drinks for 18 tables. Is giving every table the same number always fair?

Discuss:

  • What calculation would show an equal share?
  • What extra information might change the decision?
  • Could equal and fair mean different things?
  • How could mathematical evidence help organisers explain their choice?

🌱 The Living Body

Pause before solving

Before choosing an operation, pause and notice what the problem is asking you to find. You do not need to calculate immediately.

  1. Read the problem once for the story.
  2. Read it again for the mathematical information.
  3. Underline or name what you need to find.
  4. Choose an operation and explain why.

Confidence check

Choose the statement that fits you today:

  • “I need a model or worked example beside me.”
  • “I can choose an operation when I talk it through.”
  • “I can choose, calculate and check independently.”

Helpful self-talk

“I can separate the story into smaller steps. I can estimate first. A mistake gives me information about what to try next.”

Reflection

  • One answer I checked successfully was...
🛠 Final Task

Assignment: Design Your Own Community Event Plan

Create a one-page event plan that uses all four operations. You may present it as a poster, report, slide or annotated diagram.

Your plan must include:

  • a total budget between £5,000 and £20,000
  • at least two different costs that must be added
  • a subtraction showing how much money remains
  • a multiplication involving a price or quantity for several equal groups
  • a division involving equal sharing or grouping
  • one multi-step calculation
  • an estimate and an inverse check
  • a written explanation of why each operation was chosen

KS3 readiness

This task prepares learners to select operations, solve multi-step problems, use formal methods, interpret remainders and justify the reasonableness of solutions.

Final Task Rubric

Criteria Emerging Developing Secure Extending
Conceptual Understanding I can identify some operations with support. I can explain what each operation does in familiar examples. I can select and explain appropriate operations in one-step and multi-step contexts. I can compare different methods and justify why one is more efficient.
Fluency & Accuracy I can complete parts of a calculation using prompts or models. I can use written methods with occasional place-value or calculation errors. I can calculate accurately using all four operations and check my results. I can choose efficient mental or written methods and maintain accuracy in complex calculations.
Application to Problems I can attempt a familiar one-step problem when the operation is given. I can solve familiar problems and choose an operation with some support. I can solve multi-step problems, interpret the context and judge whether answers are reasonable. I can design, test and refine my own multi-step model while explaining assumptions and constraints.
Independence & Reflection I can begin with support and say when I need help. I can use a suggested strategy and respond to feedback. I can select a strategy, check my work and explain what helped me succeed. I can work independently, improve my model and reflect on how my choices affected the outcome.

Exit question: How can you prove that you chose the right operation?
